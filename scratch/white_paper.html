Executive Overview

Despite decades of progress in immunization, millions of children worldwide continue to miss life-saving vaccines. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) and UNICEF, many of these children reside in underserved, geographically isolated, urban informal settlements, conflict-affected areas, or populations facing significant barriers to healthcare access (WHO and UNICEF, 2024).

While vaccines themselves are increasingly available, one of the most persistent challenges remains planning.

Many immunization programmes continue to rely on fragmented planning processes involving spreadsheets, paper-based microplans, disconnected geographic information systems, manual population estimates, and separate forecasting tools. These approaches often limit visibility into underserved populations and constrain the ability of health systems to make timely, evidence-based decisions.

VaxPlan was developed to address this challenge.

VaxPlan is a GIS-enabled, offline-first, multi-tenant digital public infrastructure platform designed to support immunization microplanning, outreach planning, geographic intelligence, resource forecasting, supportive supervision, and programme performance management.

The platform integrates geographic information systems, population intelligence, operational planning workflows, and interoperability standards within a unified ecosystem that supports national immunization programmes from facility level to national level.

Chapter 1. Introduction

  • 1.1 Immunization as a Global Public Health Priority

Immunization is widely recognized as one of the most successful and cost-effective public health interventions ever implemented. Vaccination programmes prevent millions of deaths annually and contribute significantly to reductions in morbidity, disability, and healthcare expenditure associated with vaccine-preventable diseases (World Health Organization, 2024).

Over the past five decades, routine immunization programmes have achieved remarkable gains in child survival. Expanded access to vaccines has contributed to substantial reductions in diseases such as measles, polio, tetanus, diphtheria, pertussis, and hepatitis B. Despite these achievements, global immunization coverage remains uneven, and substantial inequities persist both between and within countries (WHO and UNICEF, 2024).

The World Health Organization (WHO) and the United Nations Children's Fund (UNICEF) estimate that millions of children continue to miss essential vaccines every year, with many never receiving a single routine vaccine dose during infancy. These children are commonly referred to as "zero-dose children" and have become a central focus of global immunization strategies under the Immunization Agenda 2030 (IA2030) framework (WHO, 2020; WHO and UNICEF, 2024). Zero-dose children are typically concentrated among populations facing geographic, economic, social, political, or health-system barriers to accessing services. These include remote rural populations, informal urban settlements, conflict-affected communities, nomadic populations, and underserved border regions. Reducing the number of zero-dose children has therefore become one of the most important indicators of equity within national immunization programmes (WHO, 2020; Gavi, 2021).

  • 1.2 The Evolution of Immunization Planning

The success of immunization programmes depends not only on vaccine availability but also on the ability of health systems to effectively plan, deliver, monitor, and continuously improve vaccination services.

Historically, immunization planning has relied heavily on facility-level microplanning. Microplanning is the process through which health facilities estimate target populations, identify communities to be served, schedule vaccination sessions, allocate resources, and monitor implementation. The approach became a central component of the WHO Reaching Every District (RED) strategy, which emphasized local planning, supportive supervision, community engagement, monitoring, and evidence-based decision making (WHO, 2009).

Microplanning remains one of the most important operational tools available to immunization programmes. However, in many settings, microplanning processes continue to rely on paper-based templates, spreadsheets, printed maps, and manually aggregated population estimates. These approaches are often labor-intensive, difficult to maintain, and poorly integrated with broader health information systems.

As countries seek to achieve the goals of Immunization Agenda 2030, there is increasing recognition that traditional planning approaches are insufficient to identify and reach underserved populations at scale (WHO, 2020).

  • 1.3 The Emergence of Geospatial Intelligence in Public Health

Recent advances in geographic information systems (GIS), satellite imagery, remote sensing, machine learning, and population modelling have created new opportunities for strengthening health service planning.

Geospatial technologies are increasingly being used to support disease surveillance, outbreak response, service accessibility analysis, facility placement, logistics optimization, and immunization planning. Organizations such as GRID3, WorldPop, the Humanitarian OpenStreetMap Team (HOT), and national mapping agencies have demonstrated the value of combining population datasets, settlement mapping, and geographic analysis to improve service delivery and resource allocation.

Population modelling initiatives such as WorldPop have enabled the generation of high-resolution gridded population estimates that provide more detailed insights into where people live, particularly in settings where census data may be outdated or incomplete. Similarly, GRID3 has demonstrated how geospatial data can support the identification of previously unmapped settlements and improve planning for health programmes operating in hard-to-reach environments (WorldPop, 2024; GRID3, 2024).

These developments suggest that future immunization planning systems should move beyond static administrative planning tools and incorporate dynamic geographic intelligence directly into operational workflows.

  • 1.4 Digital Transformation and the Need for Integrated Planning Systems

The last decade has witnessed significant investments in digital health systems globally.

Many countries have adopted platforms such as:

  • District Health Information Software 2 (DHIS2) for routine reporting.
  • Electronic logistics management information systems (eLMIS) for vaccine and commodity management.
  • Electronic medical records.
  • Digital registries.
  • Mobile data collection platforms.

While these systems have improved data availability and reporting, they often focus on specific functions within the health system. Operational planning processes frequently remain fragmented across multiple applications, spreadsheets, paper forms, and independent databases.

As a result, programme managers may have access to large quantities of data but limited capability to transform those data into actionable plans.

The WHO Global Strategy on Digital Health 2020–2025 emphasizes the need for interoperable, standards-based digital systems that support health system strengthening, improve decision making, and promote equitable access to services (WHO, 2021).

Within immunization programmes, this creates a need for platforms that can bridge the gap between geographic intelligence, population data, service delivery planning, and performance monitoring.

Chapter 2. The Global Microplanning Challenge

  • 2.1 Introduction

The effectiveness of an immunization programme is ultimately determined by its ability to reach every eligible individual with the right vaccine at the right time. While substantial progress has been made in expanding immunization coverage globally, persistent inequities continue to limit the impact of vaccination programmes, particularly among populations living in underserved, geographically isolated, conflict-affected, and rapidly urbanizing environments (WHO, 2020).

Many of these inequities are not primarily the result of vaccine shortages or inadequate technical guidance. Rather, they stem from operational challenges associated with planning, resource allocation, geographic targeting, service delivery coordination, and performance monitoring.

Microplanning serves as the operational foundation through which immunization programmes attempt to address these challenges. However, despite its importance, microplanning remains one of the least digitized and least integrated components of many national immunization systems.

  • 2.2 The Zero-Dose Challenge

One of the most significant challenges facing immunization programmes today is the persistence of zero-dose children.

The Immunization Agenda 2030 identifies zero-dose children as a critical indicator of equity because they often represent populations systematically excluded from health services (WHO, 2020).

According to WHO and UNICEF estimates, millions of children globally continue to miss routine vaccinations each year, with a substantial proportion receiving no routine immunization services at all during infancy (WHO and UNICEF, 2024).

Research has consistently demonstrated that zero-dose children are concentrated among populations facing multiple vulnerabilities, including:

  • Geographic isolation
  • Poverty
  • Population displacement
  • Conflict and insecurity
  • Weak health infrastructure
  • Social marginalization
  • Informal urban settlements

Gavi's 5.0 Strategy places particular emphasis on identifying and reaching zero-dose children, recognizing that improvements in national coverage averages alone may conceal significant inequalities at subnational levels (Gavi, 2021).

A major challenge is that many immunization programmes lack sufficient geographic visibility into where these populations are located and how they can be reached efficiently.

  • 2.3 Limitations of Traditional Microplanning

The WHO Reaching Every District (RED) strategy emphasizes the importance of evidence-based local planning and facility ownership of immunization activities (WHO, 2009).

However, in practice, many microplanning processes continue to rely on:

  • Paper-based planning templates
  • Spreadsheet calculations
  • Printed maps
  • Manual population estimates
  • Standalone supervision tools
  • Independent forecasting worksheets

While these tools remain functional, they often introduce significant operational challenges.

Fragmentation

Data required for planning are frequently distributed across multiple systems and formats.

Population data may be stored in one system.

Coverage reports may be stored elsewhere.

Maps may exist in separate GIS applications.

Session plans may remain paper-based.

This fragmentation limits the ability of health workers and programme managers to develop a comprehensive understanding of service delivery environments.

Delayed Decision-Making

Traditional planning workflows often require multiple rounds of manual consolidation and review.

Facility plans may be submitted physically or via email.

District managers may aggregate reports manually.

Provincial and national analyses may occur weeks or months after data are collected.

As a result, planning decisions are frequently based on historical information rather than current realities.

Limited Accountability

Manual workflows make it difficult to track:

  • Who developed a plan
  • Who approved it
  • What changes were made
  • When changes occurred

This limits transparency and weakens governance mechanisms.

  • 2.4 Population Denominator Challenges

Accurate target population estimates are fundamental to effective immunization planning.

Coverage calculations, vaccine forecasting, resource allocation, outreach planning, and performance monitoring all depend on reliable denominators.

However, obtaining accurate population estimates remains a persistent challenge in many countries.

Outdated Census Data

National censuses are often conducted at intervals of ten years or longer.

Population growth, migration, displacement, urban expansion, and settlement formation can substantially alter local population distributions between census cycles (WorldPop, 2024).

As a result, facility-level target populations may diverge significantly from official estimates.

Administrative Boundaries Versus Service Catchments

Health service catchments rarely align perfectly with administrative boundaries.

Communities may seek services across district boundaries.

Health facilities may serve populations from multiple administrative units.

Traditional planning tools often struggle to accommodate these realities.

Population Mobility

Migration, seasonal movement, nomadic populations, and displacement further complicate denominator estimation.

These dynamics can create substantial discrepancies between planned and actual service populations.

The inability to accurately estimate populations contributes directly to:

  • Inaccurate coverage calculations
  • Poor vaccine forecasts
  • Resource inefficiencies
  • Missed populations
  • 2.5 Geographic Blind Spots

A growing body of evidence demonstrates that geography plays a critical role in determining access to health services.

Research has shown that travel time, terrain, transportation infrastructure, seasonal accessibility, and settlement distribution all influence healthcare utilization and immunization uptake (Noor et al., 2006; Weiss et al., 2020).

Despite this, many immunization planning systems continue to treat geography as supplementary information rather than a primary planning input.

Common geographic challenges include:

Unmapped Settlements

Entire communities may be absent from official registries.

Rapid urbanization and settlement growth often outpace administrative updates.

Unknown Catchments

Facility catchment areas may be poorly defined or outdated.

Accessibility Constraints

Travel times may vary dramatically depending on terrain, season, road quality, and transportation availability.

Hard-to-Reach Populations

Remote populations frequently require specialized outreach strategies that are difficult to plan using conventional tools.

Without robust geographic intelligence, immunization programmes may struggle to identify populations most at risk of being missed.

  • 2.6 Connectivity and Digital Health Constraints

Digital health investments have expanded rapidly over the past decade.

Nevertheless, connectivity challenges remain widespread across many low- and middle-income countries.

Health workers operating in rural and remote settings often face:

  • Intermittent network coverage
  • High connectivity costs
  • Limited power availability
  • Device constraints

Several studies have highlighted the importance of designing digital health systems that function effectively in low-resource environments rather than assuming continuous internet access (WHO, 2021).

Applications that depend entirely on real-time connectivity risk excluding precisely those facilities that require the greatest support.

This challenge is particularly important for immunization programmes operating in hard-to-reach areas.

  • 2.7 Fragmented Digital Ecosystems

Many countries have invested heavily in digital health systems, including:

  • DHIS2
  • Electronic Logistics Management Information Systems (eLMIS)
  • Electronic Medical Records (EMRs)
  • Human Resource Information Systems
  • Geographic Information Systems
  • Community Health Information Systems

While these systems provide valuable functionality, they are frequently implemented as separate solutions addressing specific programme needs.

This can result in:

  • Duplicate data entry
  • Inconsistent datasets
  • Parallel reporting processes
  • Limited interoperability
  • Increased workload for health workers

The WHO Global Strategy on Digital Health emphasizes the importance of interoperable, standards-based systems capable of supporting coordinated decision-making across programmes (WHO, 2021).

The challenge is therefore not simply digitization, but integration.

  • 2.8 Why Existing Systems Are Not Enough

Existing health information systems provide critical capabilities for reporting, monitoring, logistics, and clinical management.

However, most were not designed primarily as operational planning systems.

A gap remains between:

  • Knowing what happened.
  • Understanding where it happened.
  • Determining why it happened.
  • Planning what should happen next.

This gap represents the space in which microplanning operates.

Modern immunization programmes require systems capable of transforming geographic intelligence, population data, operational workflows, and performance indicators into actionable plans.

Addressing this gap is essential for achieving equitable immunization coverage and realizing the ambitions of Immunization Agenda 2030.

  • 2.9 Conclusion

The challenges facing immunization programmes are increasingly characterized by complexity, geographic variability, population mobility, and resource constraints.

Traditional microplanning approaches, while valuable, are often insufficient to support the level of precision, accountability, and responsiveness required by modern immunization systems.

At the same time, advances in geospatial technologies, digital health infrastructure, population modelling, and interoperability standards provide an unprecedented opportunity to transform how immunization planning is conducted.

Geographic inequities remain one of the most significant barriers to achieving universal immunization coverage. While vaccines may be available within a country, access to immunization services is often determined by geography, transportation networks, settlement patterns, population distribution, terrain, and health system capacity.

Research has consistently demonstrated that geographic accessibility influences utilization of health services, particularly among rural and underserved populations (Noor et al., 2006; Weiss et al., 2020). Consequently, achieving the goals of Immunization Agenda 2030 requires immunization programmes to move beyond administrative planning approaches and adopt geographically informed decision-making processes (WHO, 2020).

The integration of Geographic Information Systems (GIS), population intelligence, and settlement analytics provides a powerful opportunity to improve planning accuracy, identify underserved populations, and optimize resource allocation.

VaxPlan was designed around the principle that geographic intelligence should be a core operational component of immunization planning rather than a supplementary analytical function.

The delivery of immunization services is inherently geographic.

Every vaccination session occurs at a specific location.

Every child lives within a specific settlement.

Every health facility serves a specific catchment population.

Every outreach strategy must overcome geographic barriers.

However, conventional microplanning approaches often rely primarily on administrative hierarchies rather than spatial relationships.

As a result, planners may have limited visibility into:

  • Communities located far from facilities.
  • Settlements not included in official registries.

  • 8.2 The Role of Forecasting in Immunization Programmes

Forecasting refers to the process of estimating future vaccine and supply requirements based on expected demand.

Effective forecasting enables immunization programmes to:

  • Prevent stock-outs.
  • Reduce vaccine wastage.
  • Improve budget planning.
  • Optimize distribution schedules.
  • Strengthen cold chain management.
  • Improve service continuity.

  • Reduced public confidence.

Overestimation may result in:

  • Increased wastage.
  • Excess inventory.
  • Financial inefficiencies.

WHO recommends that forecasting processes be based on realistic population estimates, service delivery plans, and vaccine schedules rather than solely historical consumption patterns (WHO, 2023).
